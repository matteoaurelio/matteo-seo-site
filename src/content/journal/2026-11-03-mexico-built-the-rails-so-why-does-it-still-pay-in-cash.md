It's lunchtime at a taquería in Iztapalapa, on the east side of Mexico City. A customer orders tacos for her family, MXN 250, and pays with two notes from her purse. By the end of the day, the drawer will hold three or four thousand pesos.

That same afternoon, the owner's nephew pays the stall's rent from his banking app, and the money reaches the landlord's account at another bank in a few seconds. That evening, a family in Michoacán receives money from a son working in California, and a teenager in Guadalajara buys sneakers online and pays for them in cash at a convenience store.

All four payments are ordinary in Mexico. Together they describe a country running two payment economies at the same time.

Measured by **value**, Mexico is already electronic. In 2025 its central bank's transfer system moved about MXN 592 trillion, roughly 17 times the size of the whole economy. The number is that large because it includes companies paying suppliers, employers paying salaries and financial institutions moving money among themselves.

Measured by **count**, the number of individual payments people make, Mexico is still a cash country. Eighty-five out of every 100 adults say cash is how they mainly pay for anything under MXN 500, and the amount of cash circulating in the economy doubled between 2019 and 2025.

This article explains how both can be true, and why it matters. It's the written companion to the second episode of my series **Payment Systems, Explained**, after [the first one on the Netherlands](/journal/2026-11-02-how-payment-systems-work-the-netherlands-ideal-and-wero/).

The short version: **Mexico didn't lack payment infrastructure. It lacked a reason for the person behind the counter to accept it.** On 14 December 2026, Mexico's central bank makes its biggest bet yet that it can change that.

## Two economies in one country

Start with the customer. Every three years, Mexico's statistics institute (INEGI) and its banking regulator (the CNBV) run a national survey on how adults use financial services, called ENIF. One question is simple: how do you usually pay for purchases under MXN 500, roughly a family meal at a taquería?

In 2024, **85.2% of adults said cash**.

That doesn't mean 85% of all payments in Mexico are in cash. It describes what people *usually* do for small, everyday purchases. But those purchases make up most of the payments a household makes in a week, so they shape habits more than anything else.

Size matters. In 2025, the central bank, Banco de México (Banxico), asked adults in cities which method they prefer when every option is accepted. For purchases of MXN 200 or less, 89% said cash. Above MXN 1,000, only 54% did. For a MXN 30 bottle of water, cash is fast, needs no phone signal and costs nothing. For a MXN 3,000 phone, carrying cash feels risky and a card offers protection. Mexicans aren't against digital payments; they use cash where cash is most convenient, and that's a large share of daily life.

### The other side of the counter

The customer is only half the story. The other half is the business receiving the money.

Picture Doña Lupe, who sells tamales from a cart outside a metro station in Mexico City every morning. (She's an illustration, but a very typical one.) A tamal costs about MXN 20. Her customers pay with coins and small notes. She buys masa and corn husks from a supplier who only takes cash. She has no employment contract, no social security, and her business isn't registered with the tax authority.

In Mexico, that makes her an **informal worker**, and she's far from alone. About **54% of Mexican workers** are informal, according to INEGI's employment survey. And the informal economy *grew* during the period this article covers, from 22.2% of GDP in 2020 to a record 25.4% in 2024. Mexico's digital payments boom happened alongside a growing informal economy, not instead of it.

Now think about what accepting cards would mean for Doña Lupe. She'd have to buy a card reader, pay around 4% of each sale in fees (almost a peso on every tamal), wait until the next day for the money, and create a written record of her income. Meanwhile, her supplier would still want cash. Nothing in that deal is attractive to her.

Her situation is the norm for small businesses. Microbusinesses, usually firms with up to ten employees, make up 95.5% of all firms in Mexico, and **only 13.5% of them accept cards**, compared with 51.1% of slightly larger small firms. In 2024 surveys of corner shops (*tienditas*) by the retailers' association ANPEC, 55.6% had no card terminal, 95.8% paid their own suppliers in cash, and 83.8% said cash simply worked better for them.

Put the customer and the business together and you get a deadlock. A customer who would happily pay by card often can't, because the shop doesn't take cards. And the shop has little reason to start, because most of its customers pay in cash anyway.

### The cash paradox

One more number confuses many people. If Mexicans pay less in cash, why is there so much more of it?

At the end of 2019 there were MXN 1.74 trillion in notes and coins circulating in Mexico. By the end of 2025 there were **MXN 3.545 trillion**, more than twice as much.

The explanation is that cash does two different jobs. It's a way to pay, and it's a way to keep money. To picture the second job, imagine a family in Toluca in April 2020. Schools are closed, one parent's job is suddenly uncertain, and nobody knows what the next months will bring. So they withdraw several thousand pesos in MXN 500 notes and keep them at home, just in case. They're shopping less, and paying for more things online, but they're holding more cash than ever.

Multiply that by millions of households and you get what happened: in 2020, the amount of cash in circulation grew 25% in a single year, even as people used it less to pay. Banxico's explanation is exactly that precautionary saving, mostly in large notes. And the habit stuck. In 2025, 28% of urban adults said they keep cash outside the banking system, many of them for emergencies.

So when people say "Mexico still uses cash," they mean three different things: a way to pay for small purchases, a way to save, and the way much of the economy gets paid. Each has a different cause and needs a different solution. Most of this article is about the first: why paying at the counter is still so hard to move away from cash, even though Mexico has excellent digital infrastructure.

## The system most Mexicans use without knowing its name

Here's what surprises most people outside Mexico: the country already has one of the world's largest instant payment systems. It's called **SPEI** (Sistema de Pagos Electrónicos Interbancarios), and Banxico has run it since 2004.

To see why it matters, consider the basic problem every payment between two banks has to solve. When you send money to someone at another bank, your bank can take money out of your account, but it can't put it into an account somewhere else. Something has to move the money *between the two banks*. That step is called **settlement**, and the infrastructure that does it is what the industry calls a **payment rail**.

In many countries, banks settle with each other in batches, a few times a day or the next business day. Until then, the receiving bank is effectively waiting to be paid. SPEI settles **each transfer individually and immediately**, using the accounts banks hold at the central bank. Once SPEI processes a transfer, the receiving bank really has the money. It runs 24 hours a day, every day, and in 2025 it was available 99.99% of the time.

### Following one transfer

Lucía rents a small apartment in Puebla for MXN 6,500 a month. She banks with BBVA; her landlord banks with Banorte.

What Lucía sees: she picks the landlord from her saved contacts in the BBVA app (the first time, she typed his 18-digit account number, called a **CLABE**), enters the amount, confirms with her fingerprint and gets a confirmation screen. The landlord's phone shows a notification almost at once.

What happens behind the scenes:

1. **BBVA checks the payment**: that it's really Lucía, that she has the money, and that it's within her transfer limit. Customers can set their own limit; if they don't, a default of 1,500 UDIs applies. (A UDI is a unit that rises with inflation; 1,500 UDIs is about MXN 13,200.)
2. **BBVA sends the transfer order to SPEI** at Banxico.
3. **SPEI settles it immediately**, moving MXN 6,500 from BBVA's account at the central bank to Banorte's.
4. **Banorte credits the landlord.** Banxico's rules require this within five seconds for transfers of up to 1,500 UDIs.
5. **Both sides can download an electronic receipt**, called a CEP, the digital equivalent of a stamped deposit slip.

This is an **open-loop** system, a concept from the Netherlands episode. Lucía never signed anything with Banorte, and her landlord never signed anything with BBVA. Each deals only with their own bank, and SPEI connects every bank to every other. That's why systems like this can grow quickly: when one more bank joins, all its customers can immediately pay everyone else's.

### How big it has become

In 2025, SPEI processed **7.3 billion transfers by people and companies**, 36.8% more than the 5.3 billion of the year before. That's roughly 14 times the 505 million instant transfers between banks in the Netherlands, in a country with about seven times the population.

More telling than the total is how many people use it. In the last three months of 2025, **82.9 million people, about 81% of Mexicans aged 15 and over, sent or received at least one transfer.** Divide the annual total by the adult population and you get roughly six transfers per adult per month. To picture it: the rent, a payment to the plumber, money sent to a parent, an online order, and a dinner bill split with friends. Some of those transfers are business payments, so the true personal figure is lower, but transfers are clearly part of ordinary life.

For comparison, Brazil's central-bank instant payment system, Pix, sees roughly 31 payments per person per month, and Colombia's new system reached about three in its first year. Countries count slightly differently, so treat these as rough. But they place Mexico where it belongs: far behind Brazil, ahead of a newcomer.

Most transfers are small: 94.6% were for about MXN 13,200 or less. The huge peso value comes from a minority of very large business payments; the typical transfer is more like Lucía's rent. Transfers are also getting cheaper. The average fee banks charge individuals fell from MXN 2.6 to MXN 1.5 during 2025, and three large institutions charge nothing. And SPEI is no longer just for banks: 41 of its 94 direct participants in 2025 were non-banks, including fintechs, which means a digital wallet can offer transfers without going through a traditional bank.

### Why SPEI succeeded without a brand

SPEI has no consumer logo and no advertising. Most Mexicans say "I'll send you a transfer," not "I'll pay you by SPEI." So why did it take off?

Because **people adopt a payment method when the person they're paying already accepts it.** Think about who Lucía pays by transfer: her landlord, her family, the person who fixes her laptop, an online shop. All of them already had a bank account and a CLABE. Nobody had to sign up for anything, buy equipment or pay a fee to receive money. They shared a number, and the money arrived.

That idea, acceptance first, also explains what happened when Banxico tried to bring SPEI to the one place transfers hadn't reached: the shop counter.

## CoDi: the free product nobody sold

By 2019, Banxico had a fast, cheap way for people to pay each other. The next target was where cash still ruled: the *tiendita*, the market stall, the taquería. In September 2019 it launched **CoDi** (Cobro Digital, "digital collection").

With CoDi, **the shop requests the payment** and the customer approves it. Picture Don Ramón's *tiendita* and a customer buying MXN 85 of groceries:

1. Don Ramón enters MXN 85 in his banking app, which shows a **QR code** containing a payment request.
2. The customer scans it with her own banking app, which shows the shop's name and the amount.
3. She approves, and her bank sends an ordinary SPEI transfer, settled in seconds.
4. Don Ramón's app confirms the money has arrived.

For a small shop, this looked ideal: no terminal to buy, no card network, **no fee**, and money that arrives instantly instead of the next day. For a shop owner paying 3–4% on every card sale, it should have been irresistible.

It wasn't. In 2025, after six years, CoDi recorded **4.4 million payments**, fewer than SPEI handles in half a day. On paper, around 21.8 million accounts were signed up, but Banxico counted only **279,400 accounts actually used** in the last quarter of 2025: about one active account for every 80 registered. At the end of 2025, only 1,081 merchants were using it.

### Why it failed: follow the incentives

The technology worked. The problem was that almost nobody involved had a strong reason to push it. It helps to look at each participant in turn.

**The banks.** When a customer pays by card, the bank that issued the card earns a fee on every payment (called interchange, explained in the next section), and the bank that provides the shop's terminal earns a fee too. When the customer pays by CoDi, neither earns anything. So banks had every reason to promote cards and little reason to promote CoDi. Many placed it deep in their apps, behind an extra registration step, with a different design at every bank. Banxico is now forcing all apps to show transfers in one standard way, which tells you how much that mattered.

**The fintechs.** At first, only banks could offer CoDi, so the wallets and apps winning young users couldn't distribute it.

**The merchants.** A free payment method is only useful if customers use it. Nobody was paid to visit Don Ramón, explain CoDi and help him set it up. In the card world, that expensive job of signing up merchants one by one is funded by fees. CoDi had no fees, so it had no sales force.

**The customers.** Paying a person already worked well with a normal transfer. Paying in a shop already worked with cash, and often with cards. CoDi solved a problem most customers didn't feel they had.

Research supports this reading. A 2025 study by the economist David Argente and co-authors, published by the US National Bureau of Economic Research, found that Brazil's Pix and Costa Rica's SINPE Móvil spread to every income group within about five years, helped by low costs, coordinated effort by providers and early awareness campaigns. CoDi stayed concentrated among higher-income users. In 2024, 38% of Mexican adults had heard of CoDi, and only 12.8% of them had ever used it.

### DiMo: half a fix

In 2023, Banxico tried again with **DiMo** (Dinero Móvil). Instead of an 18-digit CLABE, you send money to a **phone number**, and the app shows the recipient's name before you confirm, which helps prevent mistakes and some scams. Behind the scenes, it's a normal SPEI transfer.

DiMo signed up 16.4 million users by the end of 2025, but they made only 3.1 million transfers with it that year: roughly one per user every five years. It made paying a friend a little easier. But paying a friend was never Mexico's problem, and DiMo did nothing for the counter.

> A payment system lowers the cost of a payment. It doesn't create the reason to accept one.

That's the Netherlands lesson seen from the other side. In the Netherlands, iDEAL, a bank-run method that sends online shoppers to their own banking app to approve a payment, became the main way people pay online without any government requiring it. It succeeded because it **solved a merchant's problem**: cheap, guaranteed payments from customers who already trusted their bank app. CoDi tried to solve a merchant's problem too, but nobody was paid to bring merchants on board, and customers had no protection if something went wrong.

## Follow the MXN 250: what a card payment really costs

If CoDi didn't take off, cards are what most Mexican shops add first when they move beyond cash. To see why that's both easy and expensive, it helps to meet the companies involved. There are more of them than most people realize.

- The **issuer** is the customer's bank, which gave her the card.
- The **acquirer** is the bank on the merchant's side. It's responsible for the shop's card payments and guarantees the shop gets paid.
- An **aggregator** sits between small shops and the acquirer. Instead of each shop signing a contract with a bank, the aggregator signs up thousands of small businesses under its own agreement. In Mexico, Clip and Mercado Pago are the best-known.
- A **switch** routes the payment between the merchant's side and the customer's bank. Two switches owned by groups of large banks, **Prosa** and **E-Global**, carry a large share of Mexico's domestic card payments (more on who owns what below).
- The **card network**, Visa or Mastercard, owns the brand and writes the rules.

Two fees hold this together. The **merchant discount rate** is the percentage the shop pays on each card sale. Part of it goes to the customer's bank as the **interchange fee**, which pays the issuer for things like fraud checks, running the card and, with credit cards, lending the customer money. The rest is shared between the acquirer, the aggregator, the switch and the network.

### One taco order, step by step

Back at the taquería, the customer now taps her debit card on a small **Clip** reader, the kind you see on market stalls across Mexico. She taps, the reader beeps, she gets a receipt by text message.

Behind the scenes, the reader sends the payment to Clip, which passes it to its acquiring bank. The acquirer sends it to a switch, which routes it to the customer's bank (crossing to the other switch if her bank is connected there). Her bank checks her balance, runs fraud checks and approves, and the answer travels back to the reader. Later, the banks settle what they owe each other, the acquirer pays Clip, and Clip pays the taquería, usually after about 24 hours.

Clip charges **3.6% of each sale, plus 16% VAT (IVA) on that fee**:

| | MXN |
| --- | --- |
| Customer pays | 250.00 |
| Clip's fee (3.6%) | 9.00 |
| VAT on the fee (16%) | 1.44 |
| **Taquería receives, the next day** | **239.56** |

Out of Clip's MXN 9, the customer's bank receives the interchange. In Mexico, interchange isn't capped by law; Banxico publishes the current maximums of 1.15% for debit cards (never more than MXN 13.50 per payment) and 1.91% for credit. On this debit payment, that's up to about MXN 2.88. The rest of the MXN 9 is divided among Clip, its bank, the switch and the network, in proportions that aren't public.

One more detail matters for small businesses. A formally registered business can usually recover the VAT it pays on fees. An informal taquería can't. So its real cost is MXN 10.44, about 4.2% of the sale.

### What this means for a small café

A MXN 10 fee doesn't sound like much. Its weight becomes clear over a month.

Take a small café in Guadalajara. The following numbers are **illustrative assumptions**, not data about a real business: the café takes **MXN 50,000 a month in card payments**, the average purchase is MXN 120 (a coffee and a pastry), so it processes about 417 card payments a month, and 70% of them are by debit card, 30% by credit.

Here's what it would pay each month under different options, using list prices from late 2026 and including VAT on the fees:

| Option | What it charges | Monthly cost | Share of card sales |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clip reader | 3.6% per sale | ≈ MXN 2,090 | ≈ 4.2% |
| Mercado Pago Point reader | 3.5% per sale | ≈ MXN 2,030 | ≈ 4.1% |
| BBVA bank terminal | 2.15% + MXN 0.42 per sale, plus MXN 1,800 in one-off fees spread over two years | ≈ MXN 1,540 | ≈ 3.1% |
| Average rate banks charge restaurants (Banxico, July 2026) | 2.17% debit, 2.53% credit | ≈ MXN 1,320 | ≈ 2.6% |

The gap between Clip and the BBVA terminal is about MXN 550 a month, or roughly MXN 6,600 a year.

To see what that means, add one more assumption. Suppose that after paying for coffee, milk, rent and staff, the café keeps about 10% of sales as profit, before card fees. On MXN 50,000 of card sales, that's about MXN 5,000. With Clip, card fees would absorb around 42% of that profit. With the BBVA terminal, about 31%. Same coffee, same customers, very different outcome.

### Why the smallest businesses tend to pay more

If the bank terminal is cheaper, why do so many small businesses choose an aggregator, and why do the smallest often pay the highest rates? Some reasons are documented; others are more speculative.

**Fixed costs weigh more on small businesses.** A bank terminal comes with a contract, upfront fees (about MXN 1,800 at BBVA) and a monthly charge if the business sells below a minimum. A café selling MXN 50,000 a month on cards can absorb that. A *tiendita* selling MXN 15,000 can't: the fixed costs eat the lower percentage. Aggregators have no contract, minimum or rental, so for the smallest businesses they're often cheaper overall, even at a higher rate.

**List prices aren't what everyone pays.** Banxico publishes the average rates providers actually charge merchants, by type of business. In July 2026, merchants on aggregators paid on average 2.58% on debit and 2.89% on credit, well below the 3.5–3.6% that aggregators advertise. One plausible explanation is that larger merchants negotiate lower rates while the smallest pay list price. Banxico's data don't prove that directly; it's an interpretation, though a reasonable one.

**Informality can raise the fee itself.** Interchange in Mexico varies by type of business. Mexico's former competition authority, COFECE, found in 2023 that businesses that can't prove what kind of business they are end up paying higher interchange, and informal businesses are the most likely to be in that position.

Banxico's figures hold one more surprise. Debit cards cost merchants almost as much as credit cards (for retailers on bank terminals, 2.77% versus 2.94% on average), even though interchange on debit is much lower. You'd expect a much cheaper debit payment for the shop. It isn't, which suggests that lower costs for banks don't automatically reach merchants. COFECE made a related point in 2023, finding that average interchange in Mexico was 1.36%, against about 0.2% in Europe, where interchange is capped by law.

### Easier, not cheaper

None of this means aggregators failed. They transformed card acceptance in Mexico, and it's worth seeing how.

Compare two taquerías deciding to accept cards. The first goes to its bank. It usually needs an account with that bank, signs a contract, pays an affiliation fee and a terminal fee upfront, and may face a monthly charge if its card sales fall below a minimum. The second buys a small Clip or Mercado Pago reader, signs up from its phone, and can start taking cards almost immediately, with no contract, no minimum and money arriving within a day.

For a tiny business with irregular sales, the second option is far more practical. That's why aggregators took over. At the end of 2024, Mexico had **6.54 million card terminals**, and 70% of them belonged to aggregators such as Clip and Mercado Pago; banks ran about 21%. Mercado Pago alone reported 1.4 million active readers in Mexico in mid-2026. Aggregators reached every municipality in the country.

But there's little sign they made acceptance *cheaper*. Their list prices of 3.5–3.6% sit above the average rates banks charge, as the café example showed. And Mexico still has only 607 card terminals per 10,000 adults, compared with 1,390 in Brazil. Put simply: in a Brazilian town of 10,000 adults you'd find more than twice as many places to pay by card as in a Mexican town of the same size.

**Fintechs made accepting cards easier, not cheaper.** For a business on thin margins, that difference is a big part of why cash still feels like the better deal. Cash costs nothing to accept and the money is available immediately, even if it brings other costs: trips to the bank, the risk of theft, and errors in counting change.

## Who owns the pipes

So far we've looked at what a card payment costs the merchant. To understand *why* it costs that much, and why regulators keep stepping in, we need to look at who runs the machinery behind each payment. The clearest way is to follow one more payment, this time one that goes through a bank instead of an aggregator.

### One payment, four roles

A customer pays MXN 500 at our café with a BBVA debit card that has the Visa logo on it. The café's card terminal comes from Santander.

Four different organizations take part:

- **BBVA** issued the card and holds the customer's account. It decides whether to approve the payment. In the language of the industry, it's the **issuer**.
- **Santander** provides the café's terminal and is responsible for making sure the café gets paid. It's the **acquirer**.
- **Visa**'s logo is on the card. Visa writes the rules every bank in its network must follow: the technical standards, the security requirements, and how disputes and chargebacks are handled.
- A **switch** carries the messages between Santander and BBVA. It sends the café's request to BBVA, brings BBVA's answer back, and afterwards helps the banks work out what they owe each other so they can settle. In Mexico, the official name for these switches is *cámaras de compensación*, or clearing houses.

The important point is that **the logo on the card doesn't tell you who moves the payment.** Visa sets the rules for this transaction, but the request can travel through a switch that Visa doesn't own.

This is where the framework from the Netherlands episode becomes useful. Every payment system has three layers: the **brand** (the Visa logo the customer recognizes), the **rules** (Visa's rulebook, which binds BBVA and Santander) and the **processing** (the switch and the banks that actually move the messages and the money). In Mexico, those layers often belong to different companies.

### Who owns the switches

Banxico has authorized four card switches in Mexico. Two belong to groups of banks: **Prosa**, owned by Santander, Banorte, Scotiabank, HSBC and Invex, and **E-Global**, owned by BBVA and Banamex. The other two belong to **Mastercard** and **Visa**, authorized in 2018 and 2020. How much traffic those two actually carry isn't clear: one account says Mastercard reached 7.8% of the market within three years of starting, another says it hadn't begun operating. Prosa alone handles about 44% of the processing done by Mexico's card switches, according to the competition authority.

Now look again at our café payment. Santander, the café's bank, is an owner of Prosa. BBVA, the customer's bank, is an owner of E-Global. The two banks on either side of the payment also own the infrastructure that connects them.

### Why ownership matters

Imagine a new digital bank that wants to issue cards, or a fintech that wants to sign up merchants. To do either, it has to connect to a switch. Most of that infrastructure belongs to the large banks it's trying to compete with. The price of connecting, the technical requirements and how quickly a newcomer gets in are all set by companies with an interest in the outcome.

That's the concern Mexico's competition authorities have raised for years. In 2020, the former authority, COFECE, issued a preliminary finding that card payments lacked effective competition and recommended that the banks sell majority control of Prosa and E-Global. In 2023, it concluded that the rules for card processing favored incumbents, made entry harder and limited innovation. Banxico has since drafted new rules for the switches covering interoperability, neutrality, fair conditions for new entrants and a ban on exclusive arrangements. They went to public consultation in December 2025 and weren't yet final in early November 2026.

### How this connects to the café's fee

It's tempting to assume that whoever owns the switch sets the merchant's fee. It doesn't work that directly.

Suppose the café pays the average rate banks charge restaurants on debit cards, 2.17%. On the MXN 500 payment, that's about MXN 10.85, plus VAT. Most of it never reaches the switch. Up to MXN 5.75 goes to BBVA as interchange (1.15% is the current published maximum for debit). Santander keeps the rest, after paying Visa's network fees and the switch's processing fee, neither of which is public.

So the link between switch ownership and merchant fees is indirect. Limited competition in processing can raise costs for the banks and fintechs that depend on it, and make it harder for a newcomer to enter with a cheaper offer. And the largest slice, interchange, isn't set by law in Mexico: the banks set it, Banxico publishes it, and it flows to the banks that issue cards, many of which also own the switches.

### The Visa–Prosa deal

That background explains why one deal drew so much attention. Visa is already the dominant card brand in Mexico, with about 61% of credit cards and 64% of debit cards. But it isn't only a brand: it's also licensed to run a switch in Mexico, which makes Prosa a competitor. In December 2023, Visa agreed to buy 51% of Prosa.

On 20 February 2026, Mexico's new antitrust authority, the **Comisión Nacional Antimonopolio** (which replaced COFECE in 2025), **blocked the deal**. It gave three main reasons:

1. **Fewer independent processors.** Visa would be buying one of its main competitors in processing.
2. **Access to rivals' data.** Prosa handles payments made with every card brand, including Mastercard's, so Visa would gain a view of its competitors' business.
3. **Carnet's future.** Prosa owns Carnet, a smaller, lower-cost Mexican card brand that competes with Visa and Mastercard. The authority worried that under Visa's control, this cheaper alternative could be weakened.

These were risks the authority foresaw, not evidence of misconduct, and it judged the remedies Visa offered insufficient. Visa said it was disappointed and was reviewing its options.

The case shows something easy to miss about payments: competition doesn't only happen between the logos printed on cards. It also happens in the infrastructure behind every transaction.

### The fight over fees

Alongside the fight over the pipes, regulators have tried to lower the fees themselves, starting with interchange, the slice that goes to the bank that issued the card.

To see what's at stake, take a MXN 1,000 purchase. Today, the published maximum interchange is 1.91% for credit cards and 1.15% for debit cards. So on that MXN 1,000 purchase, the customer's bank can receive up to MXN 19.10 if she pays by credit card, or up to MXN 11.50 if she pays by debit.

In October 2025, Banxico and the CNBV proposed capping interchange at 0.6% for credit and 0.3% for debit. On the same MXN 1,000 purchase, the bank's slice would have fallen to MXN 6 for credit and MXN 3 for debit, much closer to European levels.

That proposal didn't survive. It was withdrawn in February 2026 after objections from banks, from fintech card issuers (for whom interchange is an important source of income) and from the US government. A softer draft followed on 27 August 2026: 1.30% for credit and, for debit, 0.30% with a ceiling of MXN 10.80 per payment, phased in over three years along with annual average limits. On our MXN 1,000 purchase, that means MXN 13 for credit and MXN 3 for debit once fully in place. The same draft would also require aggregators to pay merchants on the same day they receive the money. Public consultation closed on 24 September, and in early November the rule still wasn't final.

What would that mean for our café? Assume its customers' banks currently charge the maximum published interchange, and that every peso of savings reaches the café. In that best case, it would save around **MXN 390 a month**: about MXN 300 on its debit sales and MXN 90 on its credit sales.

But both assumptions are generous. The draft doesn't require anyone to pass the savings on to merchants, and aggregator prices aren't regulated at all. A café paying 3.6% to Clip may never see any of it. Lower interchange would certainly reduce what card-issuing banks earn; whether it reduces what the café pays is a separate question.

There's a geopolitical dimension too. The US government's 2026 report on foreign trade barriers criticized Mexico's first fee proposal and Banxico's proposed rules for the card switches. Who controls the pipes of Mexican payments is no longer only a Mexican question.

## Why cash still wins at the counter

Fees are part of the story, but not all of it: CoDi was free and still failed. Several other forces keep cash strong, and they reinforce each other.

### Accounts arrived, but usage didn't follow

Between 2021 and 2024, the share of adults with at least one formal financial product (an account, card, loan, insurance or pension) rose from 67.8% to **76.5%**, according to ENIF. That sounds like a success, and partly it is. But much of the growth came from accounts people *received*: accounts for collecting government benefits rose from 6.5% to 11.6% of adults, and accounts opened through fintech apps from 2.7% to 10.3%.

The World Bank's Global Findex survey, which asks different questions, gives a more sobering view: in 2025, only **41% of Mexican adults said they had made or received a digital payment**.

Picture a grandmother in rural Oaxaca. Her government pension arrives on a Bienestar card, as it does for almost everyone receiving these benefits (99.3% of 33.9 million recipients between September 2025 and June 2026). On payment day she withdraws all of it at a Banco del Bienestar branch and pays for everything in cash. The survey counts her as financially included. But she isn't a digital payer, because nobody she pays accepts anything else. Only 25.1% of rural adults say cards or transfers are accepted almost everywhere they shop, compared with 56.3% in cities. An account only helps you pay if the people you pay can receive the money.

### Nobody wants to move first

This points to a classic problem in payments, often called a chicken-and-egg problem. A shop has no reason to accept cards if its customers don't carry them. Customers have no reason to carry cards if shops don't accept them. Each side waits for the other.

Mexico offers unusually good evidence on how this deadlock can be broken. Between 2009 and 2012, the government gave about a million debit cards to poor families who received benefits through a program called Prospera. The economist Sean Higgins studied what happened next, in a paper published in the *American Economic Review* in 2024.

To picture his findings, imagine a corner shop in a low-income neighborhood. Before the program, almost every customer paid in cash, so a card terminal made no sense. Then, within a few months, dozens of the shop's regular customers start receiving their benefits on debit cards. Suddenly a terminal is worth having. That's what Higgins found: corner stores near those families started accepting cards.

Then the effect spread. Once local shops accepted cards, *other* consumers in those neighborhoods, who hadn't received a card from the program, became 21% more likely to start using cards themselves. Wealthier shoppers even moved around 13% of their supermarket spending to the small shops.

The lesson: **acceptance follows a large, simultaneous shift in demand.** When enough customers arrive at once with a new way to pay, shops adapt, and that in turn pulls in more customers. CoDi had the payment system, but it never had that shift.

### The fear of being seen

For informal businesses, there's another reason to prefer cash: every card payment or transfer leaves a record, and records can be seen by the tax authority, the SAT.

Take Mariana, who sells handmade clothes from her home in Guadalajara. (Again, an illustration.) When neighbors pay her in cash, there's no record of her income. If she starts selling through an online marketplace, the platform reports her sales to the SAT and withholds income tax from what she earns; for many individual sellers, that withholding rose from 1% to 2.5% in 2026, which is MXN 250 on every MXN 10,000 of sales. And if she simply starts accepting transfers, every payment lands in her bank account, creating a record of her income that would be there if the tax authority ever asked.

Mexico's history makes this fear understandable. From 2008 to 2013, it taxed large cash deposits into bank accounts. Banks still report large monthly cash deposits to the SAT. And since 1 April 2026, the SAT has had real-time access to transaction data from digital platforms, including how buyers paid and which bank accounts sellers use.

In focus groups with small merchants in Mexico City and Guadalajara, researchers at the Center for Effective Global Action heard some owners say they preferred cash because it "leaves fewer traces." There's no study that measures how much this fear holds back digital payments across Mexico, so its true size is unknown. But it creates a genuine contradiction in public policy: one part of the government is pushing merchants toward digital payments, while another is making digital payments easier to tax. For someone like Mariana, the first digital payment can feel like the first step toward an audit.

### Fraud, and no way to get your money back

There's also a difference between losing cash and losing money from an account. A pickpocket can take what's in your wallet. A fraudster can empty your account.

Here's a common kind of scam. Carlos gets a call from someone who says they're from his bank's fraud department. The caller sounds professional and says his account has been compromised: to protect his savings, he needs to move them to a "safe account" right away. Carlos opens his banking app and sends MXN 20,000 by transfer. Five seconds later, the money is in an account controlled by criminals, often a "mule" account opened in someone else's name, and within minutes it has been moved again or withdrawn. When Carlos calls his real bank, the answer is painful: he authorized the transfer himself.

Now compare a different fraud. A criminal steals Carlos's card details and buys a MXN 20,000 television online. Carlos sees the charge, calls his bank and disputes it. Under the card rules, his bank can reverse the payment and claim the money back from the merchant's side, a process called a **chargeback**.

The difference comes from how the two payments work. With a card, the money is "pulled" from Carlos's account by the merchant's side, and the card rules give him a way to challenge it. With a transfer, Carlos "pushes" the money himself, and once it has been sent, it's final. This is the **chain of liability** from the Netherlands episode: every payment system has rules that decide who absorbs the loss when something goes wrong. With a card, those rules often protect the customer. With a transfer, the risk sits with whoever pressed "send."

The scale is large and growing. In the first quarter of 2026, the national financial consumer protection agency, CONDUSEF, received **1.515 million complaints about possible fraud**, 31.5% more than a year earlier and about three out of every four complaints of any kind. Financial institutions refunded **24.3% of the money claimed**. Part of that rise simply reflects more people using digital channels, which gives fraudsters more opportunities. But for a customer, the message is clear: digital money that goes missing usually doesn't come back.

So far, Mexico's response has focused on prevention rather than recovery. Customers can now set a maximum transfer amount, so that larger transfers need extra checks; if Carlos had set a low limit, the scam would have been capped. And banks are under pressure to detect and shut down mule accounts. Brazil went further and built a formal process to claw back money from fraudulent Pix transfers, although even there only about 7–10% of contested money is returned.

Trust follows from all this. In the 2024 ENIF survey, almost 40% of adults said their money isn't safe in financial institutions. But trust isn't fixed. A 2021 study in the *Journal of Finance* found that Prospera families who could check their balance cheaply at ATMs came to trust their bank over time, and began saving more. People trust what they can see working.

### Money arrives as cash, and goes back to cash

Finally, much of Mexico's money starts its journey as cash. Think of Jorge, a construction worker in Monterrey who is paid in cash every Saturday. (Another illustration.) He has a bank account, but his rent, his groceries and his bus fares are all paid in cash, so there's little point depositing his wages only to withdraw them again. In Banxico's 2025 study, 28% of urban adults said they receive wages in cash.

Even money that arrives digitally often turns back into cash, because the next person in the chain won't accept anything else. Mercado Pago says its users make, on average, two cash deposits and **four cash withdrawals** a month. For many households, a digital account isn't a replacement for cash. It's a bridge back to it.

Phones, interestingly, are no longer the main barrier. In 2025, 86.1% of Mexicans aged six and over used the internet. But 79.3% of mobile users are on prepaid plans. Picture paying for your groceries with a QR code on the day your prepaid data runs out: the app won't load, and the queue is waiting behind you. Cash always works.

Cash isn't free, of course. In 2025, 12% of urban adults said they had recently given up on a purchase because nobody had change. But for many people, these costs feel smaller and more familiar than the risks of going digital.

## OXXO: the cash toll booth of the digital economy

Nothing shows the two economies meeting more clearly than OXXO, the red-and-yellow convenience store on almost every corner.

With 24,297 stores, OXXO is more than a place to buy snacks. Banks and fintechs use shops like it as agents, where customers can deposit or withdraw cash without visiting a branch. OXXO alone makes up between 41% and 48% of all banking agent locations in Mexico.

### How a cash voucher works

E-commerce depends heavily on this network. Mexicans spent **MXN 941 billion online** in 2025, 19.2% more than the year before, according to the online sales association AMVO. Most online buyers used a debit (69%) or credit card (54%) at least once, but **23% paid at least once with a cash voucher** at a store like OXXO.

Diego, 17, wants sneakers that cost MXN 1,200 from an online store in Guadalajara. He doesn't have a card. At checkout he chooses "pay at OXXO," and the store's payment provider generates a **reference number and barcode**. He shows it at an OXXO and pays the cashier in cash. OXXO confirms the payment to the provider, the provider tells the store, and the store ships the sneakers. The store receives the money days later.

The store pays for this. With Conekta, one of the larger payment providers, an OXXO voucher costs the merchant 2.6% plus a fixed MXN 3, plus VAT: about MXN 40 on Diego's sneakers. Online card payments have a similar structure, typically 3.4–3.6% plus MXN 3–4 per payment. That fixed part matters more than it looks. On a MXN 1,200 purchase, MXN 3 is a quarter of a percentage point. On a MXN 120 purchase, it's two and a half points, which is why small online sales are so expensive to accept.

For the store, vouchers have a clear advantage: once Diego has paid in cash, the payment can't be reversed, so there are no chargebacks. They also have a drawback: many shoppers generate a voucher and never pay, leaving orders hanging and stock reserved for customers who never return. I've written about [how that plays out for a business selling time-limited inventory](/payment-solutions/case-studies/oxxo-pay-cash-vouchers-time-limited-inventory/).

### Why OXXO wins either way

OXXO doesn't only make money selling drinks and snacks. It also charges a fee to move money between cash and bank accounts, and that business works whichever way Mexico goes.

Take a real example. You work in Mexico City and get MXN 5,000 of your pay in cash. You want it in your account at Nu, a digital bank with no branches. So you go to an OXXO instead:

1. You open the Nu app and generate a deposit code.
2. At the OXXO counter, you hand the cashier the MXN 5,000, plus a fee.
3. OXXO processes the deposit, and the MXN 5,000 appears in your Nu account.
4. The fee for a Nu deposit at OXXO is MXN 17.

You've just turned physical cash into digital money, and OXXO charged MXN 17 for the service. Some banks also let their customers *withdraw* cash at OXXO for a similar fee: Banorte since 2023, for example, and Mifel since March 2026. (Nu offered withdrawals too, but stopped on 16 March 2026; its deposits continue.)

That MXN 17 isn't pure profit. OXXO has to staff the counter and handle, guard and move the cash. But picture the same transaction repeated across more than 24,000 stores, every day, and you can see why it's a valuable business.

Now think about two possible futures.

**If Mexico keeps using a lot of cash**, people keep coming to OXXO to buy things, pay bills in cash and pay for online orders with vouchers, like Diego.

**If Mexico goes digital**, millions more people open accounts with Nu and other digital banks. But anyone still paid in cash needs somewhere to put that cash into their account. OXXO charges for that step.

That's the clever part of the model: OXXO can profit from the growth of digital banking without having to become a bank. Its owner, FEMSA, did build its own digital wallet, Spin by OXXO, which had 17.6 million users in mid-2026, but it paused its application for a full banking licence in March 2026. For now, OXXO's strength is the counter, not the account.

Its position isn't guaranteed. Others want this business too: Clip has said it wants to grow its own network of cash-deposit points from 28,000 to 100,000. More places competing to take your cash could push these fees down.

The bigger lesson is that going digital doesn't remove Mexico's need for physical infrastructure. Digital banks without branches need convenience-store counters to reach the people who still earn and spend cash. Which raises a question worth asking: could a chain of convenience stores become one of the most important parts of Mexico's financial system without ever being a traditional bank?

## Remittances: the money crossing the border goes digital

Mexico's largest flow of money from abroad tells the same story from another angle.

A family in Zamora, Michoacán, receives about US$400 a month from a son in California. It pays for food, school and medicine. They're far from unusual: Michoacán received US$5.4 billion in remittances in 2025, equal to 10.5% of the state's economy.

Nationally, remittances reached a record **US$64.7 billion in 2024**, then fell in 2025 for the first time in 11 years, to about US$62 billion. BBVA Research points to fewer new migrants arriving in the US, the most deportations in 12 years, weaker US job growth and a strong peso. The 2026 figures add a detail: from January to August, the value of remittances rose 2.2%, but the number of transfers fell 2.2%, and the average transfer grew from US$391 to US$409. Fewer people are sending money, but each is sending more.

### From cash pickup to bank accounts

How the money arrives is changing fast. To see why, follow the son in California as he decides how to send US$350 home this month. He has two options.

**Option one: cash at an agent.** He takes cash to a money transfer shop. Since 1 January 2026, the United States charges a **1% tax on remittances paid for with cash**, money orders or cashier's checks, so he pays an extra US$3.50 on top of the transfer fee, which for cash-to-cash transfers through stores ran about US$8–12 in 2024. In Zamora, his mother walks to a store such as Elektra, shows her ID and collects the pesos in cash.

**Option two: from his bank account.** He opens a money transfer app and pays from his US bank account or debit card. There's no US tax, because the transfer isn't funded with cash, and the fee for this kind of transfer was about US$5 in 2024. The pesos are then deposited in his mother's bank account, typically through SPEI.

The second option is cheaper and faster, and more families are choosing it. In 2024, 44.4% of remittances were paid directly into accounts. In 2025, it was **50.4%: the first time account deposits overtook cash pickup.** In the first quarter of 2026, it reached 52.7%, according to the Center for Latin American Monetary Studies. And the new US tax pushes in the same direction: since 84% of Mexican migrants in the US already have bank accounts, many can avoid it simply by changing how they pay.

At the same time, the only public payment link between the two countries is closing. **Directo a México**, a service run by Banxico and the US Federal Reserve since 2003, takes its last transfers on 20 November 2026, after a drastic fall in the number of transfers. Private companies have taken over.

### Where stablecoins fit in

Some of the private companies filling the gap use **stablecoins**, digital tokens designed to keep a fixed value against the US dollar. The crypto company Bitso describes the model: a remittance company's dollars are converted into stablecoins, moved across the border on a blockchain, converted into pesos and paid into the family's account through SPEI. The family in Zamora never sees a stablecoin, just pesos arriving.

Bitso says it handled **14.7% of US-to-Mexico remittances** between January and August 2026. That's the company's own figure, and it can't be checked independently, because Banxico doesn't track which systems remittance companies use behind the scenes.

Whatever happens upstream, the journey usually ends the same way: a five-second SPEI transfer into a Mexican account. The system Mexico built in 2004 has quietly become the last step for money arriving from abroad.

## What other countries teach, and what they don't

Mexico is often told to copy Brazil or India. That advice is easy to give and hard to use, because it skips the details. So let's look at what paying actually looks like in each country, what rules made it work, and which of those rules Mexico could realistically adopt.

### Brazil: the regulator made everyone show up

Picture a street vendor selling açaí on a beach in Rio de Janeiro. Next to the cart hangs a printed QR code. A customer opens her banking app, whatever bank she uses, scans the code, confirms, and the vendor's phone shows the payment a few seconds later. No card reader, no cash, no change. That's **Pix**, the instant payment system Brazil's central bank launched in November 2020.

Technically, Pix isn't very different from Mexico's SPEI. Both are run by the central bank, both move money between accounts in seconds, at any hour. The difference lies in the rules Brazil wrote around it.

**Every large institution had to offer it.** Banks and fintechs with more than 500,000 accounts were required to join. So the customer in Rio doesn't need to wonder whether her bank supports Pix; every major one does.

**It's free for people.** Under the central bank's rules, individuals don't pay to send or receive Pix transfers or to pay in shops, apart from a few exceptions. So customers had no reason not to use it.

**Non-banks were let in.** Digital wallets and fintech apps could offer Pix directly, and compete with banks to attract users with it.

**And merchants pay something, but not much.** According to the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), merchants pay on average about 0.22% of each payment, against 1.1% for debit cards and 2.2% for credit cards. On a MXN 100 sale, that would be 22 centavos with Pix, against MXN 1.10 for debit and MXN 2.20 for credit. That small fee matters. It's low enough to make the vendor prefer Pix, and high enough that the bank or fintech that signs up the vendor earns some money doing it. That's the incentive CoDi never had.

The result is huge. Pix reportedly handled about 80 billion transactions in 2025, and it's now Brazil's most used way to pay by number of transactions. And, contrary to many predictions, cards didn't disappear: credit card transactions in Brazil more than doubled between 2020 and 2025. Pix seems to have mostly taken the place of cash, debit card payments and older forms of bank transfer. The most likely reason is that people still turn to credit cards when they want credit or installments.

### India: apps did the selling, and the government paid the bill

Now picture a tea seller at a train station in Mumbai. Taped to his stall is a small QR code. A customer scans it with Google Pay; the next customer uses PhonePe; a third uses his bank's own app. They all work, because they all run on the same shared system, **UPI**, operated since 2016 by an organization owned by India's banks.

India's model differs from Brazil's in two important ways.

First, **technology companies do most of the work of attracting customers.** Apps like PhonePe and Google Pay build the payment experience on top of UPI and compete for users. That brought huge marketing budgets into the effort. But it also led to concentration: those two apps now handle 79% of the market.

Second, **merchants pay nothing.** The government decided in 2020 that merchants shouldn't be charged for UPI payments, and it pays a subsidy to the banks and apps instead. That's great for the tea seller. But the subsidy has been shrinking, and the industry has been pushing to let larger merchants be charged a small fee.

UPI's scale is staggering: 24 billion transactions in September 2026 alone. But India also had two shocks that Mexico can't easily recreate. In November 2016, the government suddenly declared India's two largest banknotes, the 500 and 1,000 rupee notes, no longer valid, which forced millions of people to look for alternatives to cash almost overnight. And a national biometric ID system made it much easier for people to open bank accounts in the first place.

### The Netherlands: a merchant problem solved, no mandate needed

Recall the Netherlands episode. A Dutch shopper buying shoes online picks **iDEAL** at checkout. The webshop sends her to her own banking app, she approves the payment with her fingerprint, and she's sent back to the shop with the order confirmed. The shop gets a guaranteed payment for a small fixed fee.

iDEAL handles 71% of Dutch online purchases, and no regulator required it. The banks built it because it solved a real merchant problem: cheap, guaranteed payment from customers who already trusted their bank's app.

But the conditions were very different from Mexico's. Three large banks serve most Dutch consumers, so getting the whole market on board took a handful of agreements. Informality is low, so few businesses worry about leaving a record of their sales. In Mexico, banks earn interchange on cards and have little reason to push a free alternative, and many small businesses prefer to stay invisible.

### Colombia: the closest parallel to Mexico

Colombia offers the most useful comparison, because it's doing almost exactly what Mexico is about to do.

In October 2025, Colombia's central bank fully launched **Bre-B**, a system that lets people send money instantly between any bank or digital wallet using a simple "key": a phone number, an ID number, an email or a short code. Unlike CoDi, it isn't optional: a law requires banks and digital wallets to connect. So a customer of a traditional bank can send money to a friend who only uses a wallet app, just by typing the friend's phone number.

Adoption was fast. In its first year, Colombians registered 116 million keys for 36 million customers, and moved transactions at a pace of up to about 5 million a day.

The launch also shows what can go wrong. In the first days, some interbank transfers were rejected, and some money sent to customers of one digital bank, Lulo Bank, was credited at other banks instead, because some institutions hadn't fully finished connecting. Refunds for failed transactions were reported to take weeks.

Most revealing is what Colombia's central bank concluded afterwards: technology is no longer what holds Bre-B back. Habits, merchant acceptance and fraud are. And 78.6% of Colombian adults still say they prefer cash. In other words, even with a mandate and a working system, the shop counter is still the hardest place to win.

### The lessons that travel

Put these four experiences side by side, and five lessons stand out for Mexico. For each, it helps to picture what it would mean in practice.

**1. Require participation, don't just allow it.** In Brazil, a customer never has to wonder whether her bank offers Pix. In Mexico, whether you can pay a shop by QR depends on whether your bank chose to make CoDi easy to find. A requirement removes that doubt.

**2. One brand, one QR code, one directory of aliases.** Imagine a single sticker on Don Ramón's counter that every customer recognizes, whatever bank or wallet they use. Today, Mexico has CoDi, DiMo and each bank's own "transfer" button, which is confusing for customers and shops alike.

**3. A small merchant fee, below card rates but above zero.** Return to our café. If a QR payment cost it 0.25% instead of 3.6%, it would pay about MXN 125 a month on MXN 50,000 instead of about MXN 2,000. And the company that signed the café up would still earn something for doing it. That's exactly what CoDi lacked. (The 0.25% is a hypothetical, chosen to be close to Brazil's average.)

**4. A way to get money back when fraud happens, built before the system grows.** Remember Carlos and his MXN 20,000. Brazil had to add its recovery process after Pix was already huge, and still recovers only a small fraction. Mexico can design one before QR payments spread.

**5. Expect cards and instant payments to grow together.** Brazil's experience suggests that instant payments mainly replace cash, debit and old-style transfers. They don't make credit cards disappear, most likely because people still want credit and installments.

Some things don't transfer easily. Mexico has no national ID that people use in everyday payments, the way Brazilians use their tax number as a Pix key. It's unlikely to experience a shock like India's banknote withdrawal. It hasn't decided to fund a large subsidy. And it has far more informality than the Netherlands. Those differences don't make change impossible, but they mean Mexico has to rely more on design and incentives than on luck.

## 14 December 2026: Mexico's bet

So what is Mexico doing about it? Instead of launching a new brand like Pix, Banxico is changing SPEI from the inside, through regulation. Most of the changes are due by **14 December 2026**. The easiest way to understand them is to go back to the people we've met.

**For Lucía, paying should look the same in every app.** Under a rule called Circular 9/2026, every bank and fintech app must present transfers, CoDi payment requests and DiMo phone payments with the same screens and steps. Today, sending money looks different in the BBVA app, the Banorte app and a fintech app. After the deadline, the steps should feel the same wherever she banks, and typing an 18-digit CLABE will no longer be required.

**Her landlord's phone number becomes his account address.** Banxico has told institutions to link a customer's phone number to their account on request. So after the deadline, instead of keeping the landlord's CLABE saved in her contacts, Lucía could simply type his mobile number, see his name on the screen, and confirm.

**Behind the scenes, transfers should move more smoothly.** A revamped "SPEI 2.0" is due to process transfers in parallel, so a quick transfer doesn't have to wait in line behind a slower one.

Separately, on 20 October 2026, the Senate approved a new digital payments law, the **Ley de Economía Digital**. It would let Banxico require card terminals to accept QR payments, and let the finance ministry name sectors where digital payment is required. It takes effect once it's published in the official gazette, and the list of sectors comes after that.

Here's what that could mean for our café. The Clip reader on its counter could, one day, also display a QR code. A customer without a card, or one who prefers to pay from her bank account, would scan it with any banking app, and the money would arrive through SPEI in seconds. There are already 4.6 million aggregator terminals in shops across Mexico, so no one would need to buy new equipment.

### What the plan fixes, and what it doesn't

On paper, these changes fix CoDi's technical weaknesses: inconsistent apps, account numbers that are hard to type, and no presence at the counter.

But they leave CoDi's central question unanswered: **who earns money by bringing merchants on board?**

Look at it from Clip's side. When a customer pays the café by card, Clip earns around 3.6% of the sale. If the same customer pays by a free QR transfer on the same reader, Clip earns nothing, and it may lose a card payment it would otherwise have processed. Unless someone is paid to promote QR payments, or the new law forces aggregators to switch them on and show them prominently, the option may sit unused on the reader, just as CoDi sat unused inside banking apps.

And nothing in the December package helps Carlos. If he's tricked into sending a transfer after 14 December, there's still no formal process to get his money back.

Colombia's experience suggests what to expect next. The technology can be ready on time. Changing the habits of merchants and customers takes years.

### What to watch

Three signals will show which way Mexico is heading.

The first is whether **SPEI overtakes cards in the number of transactions**. Banxico has reportedly said this could happen by the end of 2026. If it does, it will mean transfers have become Mexico's most common digital way to pay.

The second is whether **QR payments actually appear on aggregator terminals**, and whether shops like our café start using them.

The third is what Mexico does about the missing pieces: a single brand that banks and wallets must offer, a small merchant fee that pays someone to sign up shops, and a legal process for recovering money lost to fraud. Without them, Mexico will have modernized the technology without changing the incentives, which is exactly what happened with CoDi.

## What it means for each player

For a **small merchant**, the cheapest way to accept payments often isn't the one you're using. A café selling MXN 50,000 a month on cards may save thousands of pesos a year by comparing an aggregator with a bank terminal. And receiving transfers directly into your own account can cost little or nothing, depending on your bank, if you can manage the work of matching each payment to a sale.

For **consumers**, transfers are fast and increasingly free, but they carry a risk cards don't: once you send money, it's gone. Until Mexico builds a recovery process, it's worth treating a transfer more like cash than like a card.

For **banks**, the current system works well. Card interchange is a profitable business, and free instant payments threaten it. That's the root of the problem regulators are trying to solve.

For **fintechs and aggregators**, the opportunities lie where the system is weakest: checkout by transfer that automatically matches payments to orders, QR payments on terminals already in shops, protection for buyers who pay by transfer, and cash points that turn into places to pay.

For **policymakers**, Mexico shows that building the system was the easy part. The hard part is designing incentives so that someone profits from bringing merchants along, and giving people enough protection to trust it.

## The rail was never the problem

Mexico challenges the usual explanation for slow digital payments. It has had a central-bank real-time payment system since 2004, and it doesn't lack phones or even accounts. What it lacked was someone paid to bring the merchant along, a way to get money back when a transfer goes wrong, and a tax system that doesn't make the first merchant to go digital feel like the first one to be audited.

SPEI shows that Mexicans switch quickly when the person on the other side already accepts digital payments. CoDi shows that a free system nobody is paid to distribute goes nowhere. So far, the winners are young, urban, formally employed people with bank accounts, and the companies serving them: aggregators, digital banks, BBVA and OXXO. The people left behind are rural households, older adults, informal micro-businesses paying the highest rates, and fraud victims who get back about a quarter of what they claim.

Three questions will decide the next few years. Will aggregators see instant payments as an opportunity or as a threat to their card income? Will the card fee and switch rules be finalized in a form that actually changes the economics, despite pressure from banks and Washington? And will the tax authority's push for visibility slow merchants down faster than Banxico's push for convenience speeds them up?

When I go back to that taquería in Iztapalapa in a year, the question won't be whether Mexico has the technology to pay digitally. It does. The question is whether anyone has made it worth the owner's while.
