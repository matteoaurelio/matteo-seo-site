Imagine you own a small retail business in São Paulo.

A customer walks into your store and purchases R$1,000 worth of products using a credit card. The transaction is authorized, the customer leaves with the products, and you have successfully completed another sale.

But there's a problem.

You might have to wait another 30 days before receiving the money.

Meanwhile, your suppliers still need to be paid. Your employees expect their salaries, your rent is due, and you need to replenish inventory.

If you want to receive that R$1,000 earlier, you may have to pay an additional fee.

This is how a significant part of Brazil's credit-card payment industry has operated for decades.

But why?

And more importantly, what does this arrangement mean for the millions of Brazilian businesses accepting card payments?

## A decision that goes back to 1984

To understand Brazil's payment settlement practices, we need to go back more than 40 years.

In 2016, *Brazil Journal* published an account by Victor Esteves, a former executive at Credicard, describing how the country's 30-day settlement convention originated.

According to Esteves, back in 1978, Credicard offered merchants three different options for receiving their money.


| Payout period | Merchant fee |
| --------------- | ------------ |
| Within 24 hours | 8% |
| After 30 days | 6% |
| After 60 days | 4% |


Despite the significant fees, approximately 95% of transactions were paid to merchants within 24 hours.

However, Brazil was experiencing an increasingly difficult economic situation, with accelerating inflation.

And this was starting to affect the profitability of the credit-card industry.

Consumers had discovered something interesting about credit cards: they could purchase goods immediately while delaying payment until their monthly bill became due.

During periods of high inflation, this delay was particularly valuable. Money could earn interest or retain purchasing power elsewhere before the consumer paid the bill.

But Credicard was facing the opposite situation.

It was paying merchants almost immediately while waiting several weeks to recover the money from cardholders.

By 1984, according to Esteves, the financing cost associated with this arrangement was approaching or exceeding the 8% fee Credicard charged merchants.

Something had to change.

Instead of continuing to finance this gap, Credicard renegotiated its merchant contracts. Merchants would pay lower acceptance fees in exchange for receiving their money later.

Within months, more than 80% of its merchants had moved to 30-day payouts.

What began as a response to an inflation crisis gradually became a convention adopted across the Brazilian payments industry.

Brazil eventually stabilized its currency through the Real Plan in 1994.

But the 30-day payment arrangement remained.

Source: [Brazil Journal — 30 dias no cartão: uma história de crédito brasileira (2016)](https://braziljournal.com/30-dias-no-cartao-uma-historia-de-credito-brasileira/).

## How does the arrangement work today?

Let's first distinguish between two things that are frequently confused: payment authorization and merchant settlement.

When a Brazilian customer pays with a credit card, the issuer can authorize the transaction within seconds.

But authorization does not mean the money has already reached the merchant's bank account.

In a typical four-party card payment, the issuer is responsible for the cardholder, the acquirer provides payment acceptance services to the merchant, and the card network establishes rules governing how the participating institutions interact.

In Brazil, the timing of the financial transfers has traditionally been different from many international markets.

Mastercard's Brazilian rules, as described in its June 2026 arrangement documentation, allow up to 28 days for the domestic credit-card transfer from issuer to acquirer and up to 30 days for the merchant to receive payment.

Visa's April 2026 public rules provide a maximum of 32 days after processing for domestic credit transactions in Brazil, compared with five days for international credit transactions.

These are maximum periods under the applicable rules, not necessarily the actual payout time for every merchant.

An acquirer can offer earlier settlement under a commercial arrangement.

The important distinction is that **the 30-day period is not simply a technical delay caused by payment-processing infrastructure. It is part of the financial and contractual design of the system.**

And the Brazilian Central Bank has examined its consequences.

In its 2009 *Report on the Payment Card Industry*, the Central Bank found that cardholders paid their credit-card bills, on average, 28 days after making a purchase, while merchants received their funds approximately 30 days after the purchase.

The report concluded that this arrangement meant issuers did not have to bear the financing cost of the interest-free period in the same way they would under faster merchant settlement.

Interestingly, the Central Bank also modeled a scenario in which merchants received their money after two days. Its analysis found that credit-card issuing could remain profitable under that arrangement, even with the additional financing cost borne by issuers.

This was a historical modeling exercise, not proof that the same conclusion would hold under every market condition today.

But it raises an important question.

If earlier settlement is economically possible, why has the 30-day convention survived for so long?

Sources: [Banco Central — Report on the Payment Card Industry (2009)](https://www.bcb.gov.br/content/estabilidadefinanceira/Documents/sistema_pagamentos_brasileiro/Publicacoes_SPB/Relatorio_Cartoes.pdf), [Visa Core Rules (April 2026)](https://www.visa.com.br/content/dam/VCOM/download/about-visa/visa-rules-public.pdf), and [The Payments Trace — Brazil's thirty-day card payout (2026)](https://thepaymentstrace.com/insights/thirty-days-settlement-lag/).

## The cost of receiving your own money earlier

Brazilian merchants do not necessarily have to wait 30 days.

Acquirers and other financial institutions offer a service known as *antecipação de recebíveis*, or receivables anticipation.

In simple terms, a merchant can receive funds associated with future card settlements before their contractual payout date.

In exchange, the merchant accepts a discount.

This is a form of receivables financing or factoring. The transaction may involve discounting or transferring receivables, rather than taking out a conventional unsecured loan.

The Brazilian Central Bank itself explains that merchants receive their expected funds earlier, minus applicable interest and administrative charges.

How expensive is this?

Let's look at actual published prices.

### Example: PagBank

PagBank publishes different merchant fees depending on how quickly the merchant wants to receive funds from a single-payment credit-card transaction.

For Visa and Mastercard card-present payments, the published rates include:


| Settlement timing | Advertised fee |
| ----------------- | -------------- |
| Immediately | 4.99% |
| After 14 days | 3.99% |
| After 30 days | 3.19% |


These are published acceptance prices that include different payout arrangements, not an MDR to which all three rates must be added. Actual contracted rates may vary.

Now consider a merchant processing R$100,000 in eligible credit-card sales.

If the merchant chooses the 30-day arrangement, the fees amount to R$3,190, leaving R$96,810.

If the merchant wants the money immediately, the fees amount to R$4,990, leaving R$95,010.

That's **R$1,800 less for receiving the same sales proceeds earlier**.

If a merchant processed R$100,000 every month under these same conditions, the difference would reach R$21,600 over one year.

For a business with limited operating margins, that can make a significant difference.

And this is before considering other operating expenses, inventory financing costs or the effects of installment sales.

Source: [PagBank — Published merchant fees and settlement options](https://pagbank.com.br/para-seu-negocio/maquininhas/taxas-e-tarifas).

## What happens when customers pay in installments?

Brazil has another important characteristic: the widespread use of installment payments, known as *parcelamento*.

Consumers can purchase products and divide the payment into several monthly installments, sometimes without an explicit interest charge.

This creates another challenge for merchants.

Imagine a furniture retailer selling a sofa for R$12,000 in 12 installments.

Under a conventional monthly merchant payout arrangement, the retailer may receive the proceeds gradually over the following 12 months, rather than receiving the full purchase amount immediately.

The merchant has already delivered the sofa, but much of the sales revenue remains outstanding.

If the retailer wants to receive the entire amount earlier, it can negotiate the anticipation of the remaining installments.

However, the cost of anticipating several months of receivables is generally greater than the cost of advancing one installment by a few weeks, all else being equal.

And this is not a marginal feature of the Brazilian market.

According to the Brazilian Central Bank's payment statistics for the fourth quarter of 2025, installment purchases accounted for approximately **48% of the total value of credit-card transactions**, even though they represented only around 13% of the number of transactions.

This means relatively fewer installment purchases account for almost half of the total value spent with credit cards.

For merchants selling expensive products, the financing implications can therefore be substantial.

Source: [Banco Central — Retail Payment Statistics, second half of 2025](https://www.bcb.gov.br/detalhenoticia/21079/nota).

## How much money does receivables anticipation generate for financial institutions?

One of the most interesting places to look is the financial statements of Brazilian payment companies.

Consider StoneCo, one of Brazil's major merchant acquiring and financial services providers.

In its audited 2025 financial statements filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, StoneCo disclosed approximately **R$9.40 billion in interest income from prepayment of amounts payable to clients**.

The comparable figure for 2024 was approximately R$7.12 billion.

This represented an increase of roughly 32%.

It is important to understand what those numbers represent.

They are income associated with advancing funds to clients across StoneCo's operations, not the profit made from those transactions. StoneCo also incurs financing costs and other expenses.

Nor can we assume every real was collected specifically because of the standard 30-day payout period. Installment receivables are also part of the financing business.

Nevertheless, the figures demonstrate the scale of receivables financing in the Brazilian payments market.

For comparison, StoneCo's 2025 financial income from continuing operations was approximately R$10.02 billion.

Financing merchants has become a major part of the business of accepting payments, not simply an additional service offered alongside a card terminal.

Source: [StoneCo — 2025 financial statements, SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1745431/000207097926000025/stoneco_12x2025.htm).

## What do Brazilian merchants actually think?

There is also published research examining how merchants choose their acquirers.

In September 2022, UBS Evidence Lab surveyed 465 Brazilian merchants about their relationships with payment providers. The findings were published in January 2023.

The survey found that 60% identified lower merchant discount rates as a reason to switch acquirers.

But another result caught my attention.

**33% of merchants identified lower receivables anticipation fees as a reason to change acquirers**, up from 28% in the previous survey.

In other words, the price of accessing future sales proceeds was an important competitive factor, separate from the ordinary card acceptance fee.

The survey is not a measure of what all Brazilian merchants pay today, and responses were collected in 2022.

Still, it provides evidence that the cost of receiving money earlier influences merchants' commercial decisions.

Source: [UBS Evidence Lab — Brazilian Payments: Merchants More Sensitive (2023)](https://www.ubs.com/global/en/investment-bank/insights-and-data/2023/latam-merchants.html).

## What are the consequences for merchants?

Beyond the fees themselves, I think the most important consequence concerns working capital.

A merchant can be profitable on paper and still lack the money required to operate.

Consider a retailer purchasing R$70,000 of inventory and selling it for R$100,000.

On paper, the retailer has generated a R$30,000 gross margin before payment fees and other expenses.

But if most customers use credit cards and the merchant waits 30 days to receive the sales proceeds, it must finance the next inventory purchases using other resources.

That may mean relying on existing cash reserves, negotiating longer supplier payment terms, borrowing money or paying to anticipate credit-card receivables.

The merchant then faces an additional decision.

Is it more economical to pay for faster settlement or to obtain working-capital financing somewhere else?

The answer depends on the merchant's financing alternatives, profit margins, available cash and the exact fees being offered.

Smaller businesses may have less bargaining power and fewer financing alternatives than larger retailers.

This can affect their ability to replenish inventory, expand operations or negotiate with suppliers.

And there is another possible consequence: pricing.

When accepting a credit card becomes more expensive because of financing and settlement costs, merchants may attempt to recover some of those costs through their product prices.

Whether they can do so depends on competition, customer behavior and the payment methods available.

The cost of accepting cards can therefore have consequences beyond the merchant's bank account.

## Has anything changed?

Yes.

Brazil's payments industry today is considerably more competitive and technologically advanced than it was during the 1980s.

Merchants can choose among different acquirers, negotiate settlement periods and use payment methods such as Pix.

Pix, launched by the Brazilian Central Bank in 2020, allows funds to move between accounts within seconds, including outside normal banking hours.

For merchants, this offers a fundamentally different cash-flow experience compared with a conventional 30-day credit-card payout.

However, Pix does not automatically replace the consumer financing provided by credit cards, particularly when customers want to pay in installments.

There have also been regulatory changes in the receivables market.

The Central Bank introduced registration systems and rules intended to make it easier for merchants to use their card receivables as collateral or negotiate them with different financial institutions.

This matters because a merchant should not necessarily be restricted to its own acquirer when seeking financing against expected payments.

More competition can create opportunities for merchants to obtain better financing terms.

There is evidence of progress in card acceptance pricing, too.

The Central Bank reported that Brazil's average credit-card merchant discount rate declined from 2.29% in the second half of 2023 to 2.10% in the second half of 2025.

That is an industry average and does not include every additional financing cost an individual merchant might incur.

But the distinction between ordinary acceptance fees and the cost of early settlement remains important.

Sources: [Banco Central — Pix](https://bcb.gov.br/estabilidadefinanceira/pix/perguntasfrequentes), [Receivables regulation](https://www.bcb.gov.br/detalhenoticia/645/noticia), and [2025 Payment Statistics](https://www.bcb.gov.br/detalhenoticia/21079/nota).

## Some final thoughts

What I find particularly interesting about Brazil is how a financial arrangement introduced during an inflation crisis survived for more than four decades.

The technology has changed enormously.

Merchants no longer need to send piles of paper credit-card receipts to their banks. Transactions can be authorized in seconds, payment information can be reconciled automatically, and instant transfers are now part of everyday life.

Yet the conventional credit-card payout model still reflects a decision made in 1984.

Of course, providing financing has a cost. Banks and acquirers need to fund early payouts, manage liquidity and account for financial risks.

And merchants can benefit from having access to receivables anticipation when they need it.

But there is an important difference between financing that a merchant voluntarily chooses and financing that becomes necessary because of the underlying payment arrangements.

I think this is where the discussion becomes particularly interesting.

If merchants were routinely paid within one or two days, how much demand for receivables anticipation would remain?

Would the financing costs simply move elsewhere in the payments system, perhaps through higher interchange fees or other charges?

Would smaller merchants benefit more than large retailers?

And how much of the historical arrangement survives because it is economically necessary, rather than because it has become the established way of doing business?

These are not questions that can be answered by looking at processing technology alone.

They require understanding how payment networks distribute costs, liquidity and financial risks among consumers, issuers, acquirers and merchants.

**A payment can be authorized in seconds. Deciding who receives the money, when they receive it, and who pays for the time in between is an entirely different question.**
